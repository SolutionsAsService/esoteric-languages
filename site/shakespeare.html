What it is

The Shakespeare Programming Language (SPL) writes a program as a play. A title, a list of characters, then acts and scenes in which the characters come on stage and speak to one another. Each character is a variable holding a whole number, and what they say to each other changes those numbers.

Numbers are written as nouns. Every noun is worth 1, or −1 if it is an insult, and each adjective in front of it doubles the value. So a big big big cat is 8, and the sum of a big big big cat and a cat is 9. Big numbers need long sentences, which is part of the joke.

A line spoken to the one other character on stage changes that character’s value. This site runs a part of SPL; the word lists are a subset of the specification’s, and a play with other words is refused instead of guessed at.

Hello, World!

1942 characters; it prints Hello, World!↵ in 27 steps, one for each sentence spoken. Only Romeo talks. Each line gives Juliet the next letter’s code, reached from the last by adding or taking away powers of two, and then asks her to say it. A line that is only Speak your mind! repeats the letter, as the two l’s and the second o need.

How it runs

A loop, to count out letters. Juliet starts at 65, which is A, and Romeo holds 69, which is E. Romeo has Juliet speak, adds one to her, and asks whether she is now better than him; if not, the play returns to the scene. It prints ABCDE in 22 steps.

Scene I sets the two values, and scene II is the loop. The jump back is the only repetition: SPL has no loop keyword, only Let us return to. Each pass is four sentences, and the last pass skips the jump because Juliet is by then better than Romeo.
