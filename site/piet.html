Esoteric language

Piet

Programs are images. Commands are changes of colour between blocks.

By David Morgan-Mar.

What it is

A Piet program is a picture made of codels, square cells in one of 18 colours (six hues in three lightnesses) plus white and black. Touching codels of one colour form a block. The interpreter moves from block to block, and what it does depends on how far the hue and the lightness change on the way.

Numbers live on a stack: a pile where the last number put on is the first taken off. Most commands take numbers off the stack or put them on.

The language is named after the painter Piet Mondrian. This site's grid of black bars and coloured fields borrows from him too, and its colours are Piet's own.

Colour change is the command

Hue changeno change1 darker2 darker
0 hue stepsnonepushpop
1 hue stepaddsubmul
2 hue stepsdivmodnot
3 hue stepsgreaterpointerswitch
4 hue stepsduprollin (number)
5 hue stepsin (char)out (number)out (char)

Push pushes the number of codels in the block being left; that is how numbers get into a Piet program. Black codels and the edges of the image are walls; white ones are crossed without a command.

Hello, World!

The site’s build drew this program from the sentence, saved it as a PNG, read the PNG back and ran it: 77 × 21 codels, 26 colour changes, and it prints Hello, World!.

How it runs

The interpreter starts at the top-left codel and moves right. Its direction is held by the direction pointer, which this program never turns. Leaving a block whose size is the character code, with the next colour one step darker, is push: it puts the block’s size on the stack. Moving on with the hue shifted five steps and the lightness two is out (char): it takes the number back off and prints it as a letter.
